Finding a demand is easy. Finding one worth investing in is the hard part. The graveyard of startups is filled with products that solved real problems — problems that simply couldn't sustain a business.

## The Four-Dimensional Filter

Once you have identified and anatomized a demand, run it through four filters. Each dimension gets a score from 1 to 10. **Sustainability**: will this need persist for years, or will it vanish when the trend passes? Shared power banks scored high at first, but as phone batteries improved, the demand quietly eroded. **Cost-bearability**: can the solution be delivered at a price the target user will actually pay? Many healthcare innovations die here — the technology exists, but the cost exceeds what patients or insurers will tolerate.

> **Word Notes**
> - *erode* /ɪˈroʊd/ — 侵蚀，逐渐减弱。"Consumer interest eroded as cheaper alternatives appeared."
> - *graveyard* /ˈɡreɪvjɑːrd/ — 墓地（此处比喻失败企业的"坟场"）。

## Profit and Necessity

**Profit margin**: is there enough space between cost and price to sustain the operation? Grocery delivery apps discovered this the hard way — the demand was real, but margins were razor-thin, and customer acquisition costs ate everything. **Necessity**: does the demand genuinely need to exist, or is it manufactured? This is the deepest filter. Some products create artificial urgency — limited editions, artificial scarcity, fear-based marketing. These can generate short-term revenue, but they build on sand. A demand rooted in genuine human necessity is structurally resilient.

> **Word Notes**
> - *razor-thin* — 极薄的，极微小的。"Profit margins were razor-thin, leaving no room for error."
> - *artificial scarcity* — 人为制造的稀缺。"The company used artificial scarcity to drive up prices."
> - *resilient* /rɪˈzɪliənt/ — 有韧性的，能恢复的。"Demands rooted in necessity are structurally resilient."

## Case Study Scoreboard

Consider three real products. **Uber**: sustainability 9 (urban transport is permanent), cost-bearability 8 (cheaper than taxis), profit margin 5 (still struggling), necessity 8. Total: 30/40 — strong despite margin pressure. **Shared bicycles (Ofo era)**: sustainability 4 (easily replaced by e-bikes), cost-bearability 9 (cheap), profit margin 2 (devastating losses), necessity 5. Total: 20/40 — the numbers warned us, but hype drowned them out. **Smartphone**: sustainability 10, cost-bearability 7, profit margin 8, necessity 10. Total: 35/40 — a generational demand.

> **Word Notes**
> - *hype* /haɪp/ — 炒作，大肆宣传。"The hype around the product far exceeded its actual value."
> - *devastating* /ˈdevəsteɪtɪŋ/ — 毁灭性的。"The losses were devastating — the company burned through billions."

## Key Takeaways

- Four filters: sustainability, cost-bearability, profit margin, and genuine necessity
- Score each dimension 1–10; demands below 25/40 carry structural risk
- Real cases prove the formula works retrospectively — the discipline is learning to apply it prospectively

*A brilliant idea that scores 15 out of 40 is not an opportunity — it is an expensive lesson waiting to happen.*
