A discipline without a training system is just a collection of essays. For Demand Science to produce graduates who can reliably generate high-quality demands, it needs a curriculum as rigorous as medicine or engineering.

## Year One: The Theoretical Foundation

The first stage builds the intellectual toolkit. **Probability and statistics** — because every demand prediction is a bet, and understanding base rates separates analysis from wishful thinking. **Cognitive bias literacy** — to recognize when your own brain is distorting your judgment. **Systems theory** — to see how social, economic, and technological forces interact to create structural pressures. **Game theory and behavioral economics** — to model how people actually behave under competing incentives, not how they claim they will behave. These are not electives. They are prerequisites.

> **Word Notes**
> - *prerequisites* /priːˈrekwɪzɪts/ — 前提条件，先修课程。"Statistics is a prerequisite for this course."
> - *wishful thinking* — 一厢情愿的想法。"Assuming the market will grow 50% annually is wishful thinking."

## Year Two: The Anatomy Lab

Medical students dissect cadavers. Demand students dissect case studies — but not the sanitized, Harvard-style cases with neat conclusions. Real cases. Messy ones. Why did Google Glass fail despite brilliant technology? Why did WeChat succeed where WhatsApp plateaued? Students must decompose each case using the five-component anatomy model, score it against the four-dimensional quality filter, and defend their analysis against classmates who disagree. The goal is not to find the "right answer" but to build the muscle of structured reasoning under ambiguity.

> **Word Notes**
> - *dissect* /dɪˈsekt/ — 解剖，详细分析。"We dissected the failure to understand what went wrong."
> - *plateau* /plæˈtoʊ/ (v.) — 进入平台期，停滞。"User growth plateaued after the first million."
> - *cadaver* /kəˈdævər/ — 尸体（医学解剖用）。

## Year Three: Clinical Rotation

This is where theory meets reality. Students embed in real companies, real markets, real communities — not as interns doing busywork, but as demand analysts producing actionable reports. They must identify at least three unmet demands using the four systematic discovery methods, score each through the quality filter, and present a recommendation to actual stakeholders. Some students will discover demands that score 35/40. Most will find 20s. A few will score below 15 and learn the hardest lesson: walking away from a bad demand requires more courage than chasing a good one.

> **Word Notes**
> - *embed* /ɪmˈbed/ — 嵌入，深入其中。"Journalists embedded with military units to report from the front lines."
> - *stakeholder* /ˈsteɪkhoʊldər/ — 利益相关者。"The recommendation must convince stakeholders, not just professors."

## Graduation Criteria

A graduate of Demand Science must demonstrate three capabilities. First, **analytical fluency**: given any product or service, decompose its demand structure in under ten minutes. Second, **discovery reliability**: produce at least five scored demands per quarter using systematic methods, with an average quality score above 25/40. Third, **decision courage**: present a case where they identified a demand, scored it honestly, and chose not to pursue it — because the numbers said no, even when their gut said yes.

> **Word Notes**
> - *fluency* /ˈfluːənsi/ — 流利，熟练。"Analytical fluency means the framework becomes second nature."

## Key Takeaways

- Year 1: theoretical foundation (probability, biases, systems theory, game theory)
- Year 2: case dissection using the anatomy model and quality filter
- Year 3: real-world clinical rotation producing scored demands for actual stakeholders
- Graduation requires analytical speed, discovery output, and the courage to walk away from bad demands

*The final exam is not a test on paper. It is a demand you discovered, scored, and decided on — with real consequences either way.*
