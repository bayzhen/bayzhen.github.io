Imagine a dungeon where every room is a dead end. The doors lead nowhere. The treasure chest floats in mid-air, two meters above the floor. There is no path from the entrance to the exit. This is what happens when you let pure randomness build a game level. It looks broken, because it is broken. Procedural generation is not about being random. It is about being random with rules.

## The Problem with Pure Randomness

A truly random level generator might place a wall at any tile with a 50% chance. The result is noise — not the useful Perlin kind, but visual garbage. In a 2014 GDC talk, Spelunky creator Derek Yu explained that his first dungeon generator produced unplayable results roughly 90% of the time. Rooms had no exits. Platforms were unreachable. Players spawned inside solid rock. Randomness alone cannot guarantee a solvable level.

The fix is simple in concept: add rules. Every room must have at least one exit. Every exit must connect to another room. The player must always have a path from start to finish. These constraints transform chaos into playable content.

> **Word Notes**
> - *constraint* /kənˈstreɪnt/ — 约束，限制条件。"The constraint ensures every room has at least one exit."
> - *spawn* /spɔːn/ — （游戏中）生成，出现。"Players spawn at a random safe location on the map."

## Seeds: Reproducible Randomness

Computers do not generate true randomness. They use algorithms called pseudorandom number generators (PRNGs). A PRNG takes a starting number — a seed — and produces a sequence of values that look random but are entirely deterministic. The same seed always gives the same sequence.

This is why Minecraft players share seeds. Seed `12345` always generates the same world. A YouTuber finds a beautiful mountain next to a village, shares the seed, and millions of players can visit the exact same landscape. Speedrunners exploit this property too. They memorize specific seeds to practice the same layout over and over.

In code, reproducibility is straightforward:

```python
import random
random.seed(42)
# These values are identical every time
room_count = random.randint(5, 12)   # always 11
enemy_type = random.choice(["goblin", "skeleton", "bat"])  # always "bat"
```

> **Word Notes**
> - *deterministic* /dɪˌtɜːrmɪˈnɪstɪk/ — 确定性的，结果可预测的。"A deterministic algorithm produces the same output for the same input."
> - *reproducibility* /ˌriːprəˌdjuːsəˈbɪləti/ — 可重现性。"Reproducibility is essential for debugging procedural content."

## Constraints in Practice: Spelunky's Approach

Spelunky's level generator is a masterclass in constrained randomness. It works in three steps. First, it divides the level into a 4x4 grid of rooms. Second, it carves a guaranteed path from the top row to the bottom row, choosing randomly between going left, right, or down. Third, it fills the remaining rooms with random templates.

The critical rule: the main path is always walkable. A player can always reach the exit without any special items. Optional side rooms may contain treasure or traps, but they are never required. This design means every seed produces a completable level — no exceptions.

## Hades: Randomness at a Higher Level

Not all procedural generation happens at the tile level. In Hades, the rooms themselves are hand-designed. The randomness controls which rooms appear and in what order. The game picks from roughly 80 room layouts per biome. It tracks which rooms you have seen recently and avoids repeating them. It also adjusts difficulty based on your current health and weapon.

This is the key distinction between random and procedural. Random means unpredictable. Procedural means generated by a process — a process with rules, memory, and intent. Hades feels fresh every run not because its rooms are random, but because its selection algorithm is clever.

> **Word Notes**
> - *biome* /ˈbaɪoʊm/ — 生物群落（游戏中常指区域/关卡主题）。"Each biome in the game has its own enemy types and visual style."
> - *intent* /ɪnˈtent/ — 意图，目的。"Good procedural generation encodes designer intent into its rules."

## The Golden Rule

The best procedural systems share one principle: guarantee the critical path, then randomize everything else. The player must always be able to win. The world must always make sense. Within those boundaries, chaos is welcome. The rules are not restrictions — they are what make the randomness meaningful.

*True randomness is easy. The hard part is teaching the machine which dice rolls to keep and which to throw away.*
