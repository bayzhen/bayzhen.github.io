You have three bullets left. The monster is in the next room. You do not know if three is enough.

That moment — that specific, stomach-dropping calculation — is what survival horror is designed to produce.

## The Scarcity Principle

Most action games give you enough ammunition to feel powerful. Survival horror does the opposite. Resources are deliberately thin: bullets are rare, health items cost inventory space, and saving the game requires a physical item you might not have.

This scarcity forces real decisions under pressure. Do you fight the zombie blocking the corridor, or save the bullet for later? There is no correct answer. The uncertainty is the point.

> **Word Notes**
> - *scarcity* /ˈskeəsɪti/ — 稀缺性，匮乏。"Water scarcity is a growing global problem."
> - *deliberately* /dɪˈlɪbərətli/ — 故意地，蓄意地。"The clue was deliberately hidden."
> - *inventory* /ˈɪnvəntri/ — （游戏中的）背包，物品栏；也指库存。"Check your inventory for spare keys."

## Pacing and Dread

Speed matters in survival horror — and slow is better. When your character moves cautiously and cameras show only part of the room, tension builds before the monster even appears. Dread, not shock, is the primary emotion.

Resident Evil mastered this. Fixed camera angles in the early games hid threats just off-screen. You heard something before you saw it. By the time the creature appeared, your heart was already racing.

> **Word Notes**
> - *dread* /dred/ — 恐惧，忧虑（通常指预感到的恐惧）。"She felt a sense of dread as she opened the door."
> - *cautiously* /ˈkɔːʃəsli/ — 谨慎地，小心翼翼地。"He cautiously peered around the corner."

## The Psychology of Safe Fear

Research suggests that controlled fear — fear experienced in a safe environment — can actually reduce stress. The body releases adrenaline, works through the threat response, and then recovers. It is, in a strange way, therapeutic.

This is why players return to survival horror even after finishing it. The fear feels real, but the stakes are not. You can always reload a save. The monsters cannot actually reach you.

> **Word Notes**
> - *adrenaline* /əˈdrenəlɪn/ — 肾上腺素；也比喻"兴奋感"。"The race gave him an adrenaline rush."
> - *therapeutic* /ˌθerəˈpjuːtɪk/ — 有疗愈效果的。"Some people find cooking therapeutic."

## Key Takeaways

- Survival horror works by creating scarcity, forcing real decisions under pressure.
- Slow pacing and limited visibility build dread before threats even appear.
- Controlled fear in a safe environment is psychologically satisfying — which is why players keep coming back.

*The best horror games do not just scare you. They make you want to be scared again.*
