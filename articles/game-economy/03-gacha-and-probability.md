A player sits in front of her phone at 2 a.m. She has saved 14,400 Primogems over two months. Her hands tremble. She taps the golden wish button ten times. No five-star character. She taps again. Nothing. On her 76th pull, a golden meteor streaks across the screen. She screams.

This is gacha. And behind the drama, there is cold, precise math.

## The Base Rate Trap

Most gacha games give top-tier items a very low base rate. In Genshin Impact, the chance of pulling a five-star character is 0.6% per attempt. Zenless Zone Zero uses a similar structure. At 0.6%, you would need about 167 pulls on average to see one success — if no other system existed.

But 0.6% is not the whole story. Game designers layer additional mechanics on top of the base rate. These mechanics keep players hopeful and spending.

## Soft Pity and Hard Pity

The most important mechanic is the pity system. It guarantees a rare item after a certain number of failed pulls.

In Genshin Impact, there are two layers. Soft pity starts at pull 74. From that point, the five-star rate jumps from 0.6% to roughly 6% per pull. The probability climbs higher with each attempt. Hard pity hits at pull 90 — you are guaranteed a five-star no matter what.

The result? Most players get their five-star between pulls 74 and 80. The true expected value is about 62 pulls per five-star, not 167. This is much better than the base rate suggests.

Zenless Zone Zero follows a nearly identical model. Soft pity begins around pull 75, and hard pity triggers at pull 90.

## The Psychology of Near-Misses

Gacha systems exploit a cognitive bias called the near-miss effect. When you pull a four-star item at pull 72 — just two pulls before soft pity — your brain interprets it as "almost winning." You feel close. You pull again.

Designers also use visual cues. A rainbow-colored loading screen. A dramatic camera angle. A slow reveal animation. Even failed pulls feel exciting, because the presentation suggests you were close to something great.

This is not an accident. Every animation is calibrated to keep you engaged.

## Expected Value: Doing the Math

Smart players calculate expected value before spending. Here is a simplified example.

Suppose a five-star character costs about 80 pulls on average (accounting for soft pity). Each pull costs 160 Primogems. That means one character costs roughly 12,800 Primogems. A free-to-play player earns about 60 pulls per patch cycle. A paying player can buy 6,480 Primogems for about $100.

The question becomes simple: is this character worth $200 to you? Expected value turns emotional decisions into rational ones.

## Regulation and Transparency

China changed the industry in 2017. Regulators required all games to disclose exact gacha probabilities. This rule forced developers to publish their drop rates publicly. Japan and South Korea followed with similar guidelines.

Transparency helps players, but it does not eliminate the emotional pull of gacha. Even when you know the odds are 0.6%, the golden light still feels personal. The math is public. The psychology remains private.

## Why Games Choose Gacha

Gacha works because it aligns three forces. Players get hope — every pull could be the one. Designers get engagement — players log in daily to earn free pulls. Companies get revenue — a small percentage of players spend thousands.

The model is elegant and controversial. It funds massive game worlds with free access. It also creates habits that some players cannot control.

*The odds are always printed on the label. But nobody reads the label when the stars are falling.*
