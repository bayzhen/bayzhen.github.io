In 2013, Blizzard shut down Diablo III's real-money auction house. Players had been trading legendary swords for actual dollars. The economy collapsed within months. Gold became worthless. Rare items flooded the market. Players quit because finding loot — the core joy of the game — no longer felt rewarding. A virtual economy had broken in exactly the way real economies do: through unchecked inflation.

## Virtual Worlds, Real Rules

Game economies follow the same principles as real ones. Supply and demand determine prices. When too much currency enters the system, prices rise. When rare items become common, their value drops. The difference is scale and speed. In the real world, inflation builds over years. In a game, it can spiral out of control in weeks.

> **Word Notes**
> - *inflation* /ɪnˈfleɪʃn/ — 通货膨胀。"Unchecked gold farming caused severe inflation in the server's economy."
> - *spiral* /ˈspaɪrəl/ — 螺旋式上升或恶化。"Prices spiraled upward after the exploit was discovered."

## The Gold Farming Problem

Every time a player kills a monster, the game creates gold from nothing. This is called a "faucet" — a source that pumps currency into the world. In World of Warcraft, millions of players kill millions of monsters every day. Bots make the problem worse. Automated programs can farm gold 24 hours a day without rest. In WoW's early years, some servers saw gold supplies double every few months. A health potion that once cost 5 gold now cost 500. New players could not afford basic supplies. Veterans hoarded goods and speculated on prices like Wall Street traders.

## When Duplication Breaks Everything

Inflation from farming is gradual. Duplication bugs are catastrophic. In 2003, a glitch in Ragnarok Online let players copy any item infinitely. The economy was destroyed overnight. Rare weapons that once took months to earn suddenly appeared by the thousands. Trust in the game's currency vanished. Players turned to direct barter — trading item for item — because money meant nothing.

> **Word Notes**
> - *catastrophic* /ˌkætəˈstrɒfɪk/ — 灾难性的。"The duplication exploit had a catastrophic impact on server stability."
> - *barter* /ˈbɑːrtər/ — 物物交换。"When currency fails, people revert to barter systems."

EVE Online faced a similar crisis in 2010. A blueprint duplication bug flooded the market with capital ships worth billions of ISK. The developers at CCP Games had to manually track and delete every duplicated item — a process that took weeks and shook player confidence.

## Game Designers as Central Bankers

This is why modern game designers think like economists. They must balance faucets (gold from quests, monster drops, daily rewards) against sinks (repair costs, auction fees, consumable items). If faucets outpace sinks, inflation follows. If sinks are too aggressive, new players feel punished.

EVE Online employs a full-time economist. The game publishes monthly economic reports with charts tracking money supply, trade volume, and price indices — just like a central bank. World of Warcraft introduced the WoW Token in 2015, letting players exchange real money for game time. This created a floating exchange rate between gold and dollars, effectively importing real-world monetary policy into Azeroth.

> **Word Notes**
> - *consumable* /kənˈsjuːməbl/ — 消耗品。"Health potions and scrolls are typical consumable items in RPGs."
> - *monetary* /ˈmʌnɪteri/ — 货币的，金融的。"The Fed adjusts monetary policy to control inflation."

## Key Takeaways

- Virtual economies obey the same supply-and-demand rules as real ones, just faster
- Gold farming and duplication bugs are the two biggest threats to economic stability
- Successful games balance currency faucets and sinks like a central bank manages money supply

*Every game world is a tiny country, and every game designer is its reluctant central banker.*
